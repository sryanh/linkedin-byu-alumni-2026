Your LinkedIn guide

Everything from the talk, plus the parts we didn't have time for.

Do these three things today

  1. Connect in the room

    Pull out your phone during the conversation. “Can I find you on LinkedIn real quick?” is a completely normal thing to say at an event like this. If that feels awkward, keep a notesheet in your pocket instead and write down their name, their company, and two or three things you talked about.

  2. Always add a note

    Never send a blank request. One specific sentence about what you actually talked about is what makes you findable three months from now.

  3. Follow up within 48 hours

    One short message while they still remember your face. Reference the conversation and ask one small question. Don't attach a resume and don't ask for a job.

Set up a profile worth looking at

Alumni will look you up after today. Your profile's job is to survive a recruiter keyword search and back up a referral — not to replace your GitHub.

Headline
Not “Student at BYU.” This is the most heavily weighted field in recruiter search, so use it: CS student at BYU | Backend & distributed systems | Seeking Summer 2027 SWE internship
Photo
Plain background, good light, shoulders up, you smiling. A decent phone photo is fine. No photo is not.
Banner image
Swap the default grey for anything — campus, a skyline, a simple color. Takes 30 seconds and instantly looks more deliberate.
About
Three or four sentences, first person. What you study, what you're drawn to, what you're looking for. Not a resume summary.
Experience
Retail, campus jobs, and club roles all count. Write what you accomplished, not what you were assigned. Numbers help.
Skills
Search filters, not decoration. List the actual technologies — Python, React, Go, PostgreSQL — not “programming.” Recruiters filter on exact terms.
Featured section
Sits near the top and almost no student uses it. Your GitHub, personal site, or a project demo goes here — for CS it's the most valuable field on the page after your headline.
Custom URL
Two minutes to change, and it's what goes on your resume instead of a string of random digits.

Settings most students never find

Turn off activity broadcasts before you edit
Otherwise every tweak notifies your whole network. Settings → Visibility → Share profile updates with your network. Turn it back on when you're done.
Private browsing mode
You can view profiles anonymously — useful when you're researching an interviewer. Trade-off: while private, you lose your own “who viewed your profile” data.
“Open to Work” has two versions
Recruiters-only (discreet) or the green photo frame (public). Most people don't know the discreet option exists — it matters if you're currently employed.
Job alerts
Save a search once and let LinkedIn email you new postings daily instead of checking manually.

Finding BYU alumni who do what you want to do

The single most useful feature on LinkedIn for a student, and almost nobody knows it's there.

  1. Search Brigham Young University and open the school page — not a person, the university's own page.

  2. Click the Alumni tab. On mobile it may be labeled People.

  3. Filter what they do to Engineering and where they work to a company you want. You'll almost always find BYU CS grads there — and a shared school is a legitimate reason to reach out cold.

  4. Sort by graduation year to find people three to five years ahead of you. They remember what recruiting was like and they're far more likely to reply than a senior executive.

Messages you can copy

Short, specific, no ask in the first message. That gets replies; everything else gets ignored.

Connection note for someone you met today

Great talking with you about what code review actually looks like on your team today — thanks for the advice on summer internships. Glad we connected.

Cold outreach to alumni at a company you want

Hi [Name], I'm a junior in CS at BYU. I found you through the BYU alumni page and saw you're a [role] at [company] — that's the kind of work I'm aiming for. Would you be open to 15 minutes to hear how you got there? Happy to work around your schedule.

Following up after a conversation

Hi [Name], thanks again for the time last week — the point about going deep on one project instead of shipping five shallow ones really changed what I'm building next. I started [project] because of it. I'll keep you posted.

Messaging a recruiter about a specific role

Hi [Name], I applied for the [exact job title] role (req #[number]) last week. I'm a [year] in CS at BYU, and [one specific reason you fit — something you shipped, relevant coursework, a past internship]. Happy to send my GitHub over if that's useful.

Using LinkedIn for the actual job search

Recruiting starts absurdly early
Postings for next summer's SWE internships often open in August and September. A profile that's ready in the fall beats a perfect one in March.
Referrals are the whole game
A referral from someone inside is what gets a human to actually open your application. That is what the alumni search above is for.
Apply in the first few days
Postings collect hundreds of applicants fast, and many are reviewed roughly in the order they arrive.
Skip Premium for now
The free tier does everything on this page. Save it for when you're actively interviewing and want InMail.

If you only do one thing: connect with the people you talked to today, with a note, before the day is over. The rest of this page still works next week — that part doesn't.
